What five developer doc sites were missing

A read-only HTTP snapshot taken 27 November 2026. No account, no crawler access, no paid tool.

This snapshot does not tell you how an answer engine ranks a site. It tells you something narrower and more ordinary: how often the cheap, machine-readable signals are absent, even on well-maintained documentation sites. The same check was run against five public home pages and the exact URLs /llms.txt, /robots.txt and /sitemap.xml, plus home-page HTML for a title, description, canonical link, Open Graph tags, JSON-LD blocks and H1 count.

Counts are from static HTML fetched once at the stated time. A 404 at one exact path does not prove that a site has no other index, and some sites serve different files to different clients. Missing JSON-LD on the home page says nothing about structured data deeper in the site. A robots.txt that does not name GPTBot, ClaudeBot or PerplexityBot is often a policy choice, not an accident. llms.txt is a convention being adopted gradually, not a standard failure.

How to check your own site

Open your site's source and look at five things: a root llms.txt that summarises your real pages; a robots.txt that states a deliberate policy for named crawlers and points to a live sitemap; home-page JSON-LD naming your product or organisation; a descriptive title and meta description with one H1; and server-rendered text that survives JavaScript being switched off.

A weighted score from these checks is a triage tool, not an industry standard. It measures readiness signals, not rankings. Nobody can guarantee that an AI will cite a site, and blocking a crawler can be a deliberate choice — paywalls and licensing are real trade-offs.
